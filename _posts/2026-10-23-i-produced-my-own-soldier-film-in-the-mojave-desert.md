## I made it happen myself

This wasn't a booking. Nobody called me in.

I **produced** it. That's the key word in the post: "self-produced."

It wasn't my first time, either. My first producing credit was [For All the World](/2026/10/20/for-all-the-world-the-first-film-i-ever-produced/). This was "another" one.

Here's the thing about acting. You can wait for the role you want. Or you can build it.

I love soldier roles. So I made one.

## The look

Look at the photo up top.

Short hair. Dirt on the face. Cuts on the forehead. Sweat. A worn-in plate carrier.

The light is low and warm. The sky goes from blue to gold. Behind me, the desert just keeps going.

You can't fake that horizon on a stage. You have to drive out to it.

## Who is Damien Bray?

Damien is a stunt pro. A serious one.

His IMDb page says he's known for **Dunkirk** (2017), **Deadpool** (2016) and **Sicario** (2015).

He comes from a stunt family, too. His father is **Ben Bray**, a stunt coordinator and second unit director.

So when a guy like that brings the weapons and gives you his day, you say thank you. In public.

## Why the Mojave?

Because the Army thinks so too.

The Mojave is a big desert. It covers about **54,000 square miles** across California, Nevada, Arizona and Utah. It's called the "high desert," since most of it sits between **2,000 and 4,000 feet** up.

It's also one of America's most important training grounds for real soldiers.

**1942.** The country was at war. The Army set up the **Desert Training Center** in the Mojave and Sonoran deserts.

Its first commander was **Major General George S. Patton Jr.** He was a Southern California native. He knew this desert from his youth.

His job: get American troops ready to fight in the deserts of **North Africa**. The center was activated **April 1, 1942**. Patton led it from **April to August 1942**.

It became the largest military training ground in the history of military maneuvers. It closed on **July 1, 1944**.

![Black-and-white 1942 photo of rows of light tanks with soldiers in the hatches crossing desert scrub while two bombers fly low overhead](/assets/images/2026-10-23/camp-iron-mountain-1942.jpg)
*Training at Camp Iron Mountain, part of the Desert Training Center, 1942. Public domain, via the [Bureau of Land Management California on Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Training_at_Camp_Iron_Mountain,_1942.jpg).*

## The Army never left

The Mojave is still a training ground today.

**Fort Irwin** sits in the Mojave, about **37 miles** northeast of Barstow, California.

On **October 16, 1980**, the Army officially activated the **National Training Center** there. It has more than **1,000 square miles** of room to maneuver.

Units come there to train against a tough "enemy" force. That role is played by the **11th Armored Cavalry Regiment**, the Blackhorse.

So when I put on a plate carrier and walk across Mojave rock, I'm on ground real soldiers have trained on for more than **80 years**. Not on the same spot. But in the same desert.

That's why it looks right on camera. It *is* soldier country.
